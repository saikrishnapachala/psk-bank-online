The short answer

Fiserv keeps the books. The core (Premier, DNA or the cloud-native Finxact) holds every account and balance and posts every debit and credit. That is the part our demo calls today.

Fiserv can also connect you to the payment networks (ACH, wires, real-time payments, Zelle, bill pay), but each of those is a separate product you contract for. Banking Hub's account APIs alone don't send money to other banks.

PSK Bank owns every decision and the risk. As the licensed bank, you decide who can send what, screen for fraud and sanctions, hold enough money at the Federal Reserve to settle, and answer for mistakes. Fiserv runs the machinery; it doesn't carry your liability.

Money between banks never travels core to core. It moves between the two banks' accounts at the Federal Reserve. Each core just records its side.

Journey A

Paying another PSK Bank customer

This is what the demo does today: Alex sends money to Jordan. Both accounts live in the same Fiserv core, so no network is involved. One API call moves the money on both sides at once.

Clearing accounts let reconciliation match the core to the Fed statement every day.

Checks PSK must run before it sends

  • Sanctions screening of the payee against OFAC lists (required).
  • Fraud scoring: new payee, new device, unusual amount, scam patterns.
  • Payee verification: does the name match the account? Account validation services can check.
  • Limits: per payment, per day, cooling-off period for new payees.
  • Liquidity: enough money in PSK's Fed account to settle.

Why the checks matter so much here: wires and RTP payments are final. If a customer is tricked into sending $500,000, there is no network button to get it back. PSK can only ask the other bank to return it.

Journey C

Linking a Chase account and pulling in $1 million

Plaid's job ends once it has proved the customer owns the Chase account and shared its numbers and balance. Moving the money is a separate step, and PSK has two ways to do it.

The risk in pulling money

A consumer can tell Chase the debit was unauthorized for up to 60 days, and Chase will send it back. If PSK has already let the customer spend the $1M, PSK is left short. That's why pull transfers come with lower limits and holds on the incoming funds.

The better option for $1 million

Have the customer push it: send a wire or RTP payment from Chase to their PSK account. It's final, it arrives in minutes, and Chase does the fraud checks. PSK shows its own account and routing number in the app to make that easy. Keep ACH pulls for smaller, regular top-ups.

Journey D

Sending the Chase money to someone else, or paying a bill

PSK Bank can't instruct Chase to pay a third party. Linking an account with Plaid only lets PSK read it and pull money into PSK. So there are two honest options:

Option 1: pay from Chase directly

The customer uses the Chase app. Chase is the sending bank, runs its own checks, and carries the risk. PSK isn't involved.

Option 2: bring it to PSK, then send

Pull or push the money into PSK (Journey C), then send it out as in Journey B, or pay a bill through PSK's bill pay. Two hops, but the customer stays in PSK Bank Online.

Paying bills from PSK

Bill pay is its own service. Fiserv's bill payment product (the former CheckFree network) knows thousands of billers. PSK's app collects the bill, PSK debits the customer's account, and the bill pay provider pays the biller electronically, or mails a check when the biller isn't connected.

One sentence for the team: Fiserv is our ledger and our plumbing; PSK Bank is the bank. Every rule about who may move money, and every loss when something goes wrong, sits with us.

The setup I'd build for a brand-new bank

With no legacy to work around, aim for real-time first, one payments service that knows every rail, and the core kept as the single source of truth.

Choices that pay off

  • Real-time rails first: RTP and FedNow for most payments, wires for very large or international ones, ACH for payroll, bills and cheap batch.
  • One payment state machine (created, checked, held, sent, settled, posted, or returned) so every rail is tracked and reported the same way.
  • ISO 20022 everywhere: Fedwire, FedNow and RTP all use it; one message model for the whole bank.
  • Cloud-native core: Fiserv's Finxact is built for API-first banks; Premier and DNA work too.

For customers with $10 million each

  • Deposit insurance: the FDIC covers $250,000 per depositor per ownership category, so most of a $10M balance is uninsured. Reciprocal deposit networks spread it across banks to keep it covered.
  • Liquidity: ten customers holding $100M means one $5M wire is 5% of deposits. Treasury needs live visibility of the Fed account.
  • Relationship-level controls: named approvers and call-backs for large payments, not just app limits.

Questions the team will ask

Does Fiserv send the money?

Its payment products build and submit the messages, if PSK contracts for them. The money itself settles through PSK's account at the Federal Reserve.

Is Plaid a payment network?

No. Plaid confirms the account and shares its details. PSK moves the money over ACH, or the customer pushes it from Chase.

Why not use Zelle for $500k?

Zelle is built for everyday person-to-person payments with low limits. Large payments go by wire or RTP.

What if the friend's bank isn't on RTP?

Fall back to a wire. The payments service picks the rail from the amount, the receiving bank and the time of day.

Who pays back a scam loss?

For a payment the customer authorized, usually nobody is required to, which is why PSK's checks before sending are the real protection. For an unauthorized payment, consumer protection rules put most of the loss on PSK.

Do we need a Fed account?

Yes, or a correspondent bank that settles for us. Every external payment ends there.
